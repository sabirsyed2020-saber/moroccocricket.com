When World Cricket Came to Morocco

The story of the 2002 Morocco Cup and Tangier's forgotten cricket stadium

Published: October 2026 | Morocco Cricket History

Imagine watching Pakistan, South Africa and Sri Lanka compete in international one-day cricket — not in London, Mumbai or Colombo, but in Tangier, Morocco.

It sounds like an unlikely chapter in cricket history. Yet in August 2002, it happened.

For ten days, Tangier hosted the Morocco Cup, a three-team international tournament that brought some of the game's leading players to North Africa. The competition offered a glimpse of what cricket in Morocco might become: an international destination for the sport and a place where local players could develop a lasting cricket culture.

More than two decades later, the story remains worth remembering.

A cricketing ambition takes shape

Cricket did not suddenly appear in Morocco in 2002. The game had already been played by expatriate communities, including British and Pakistani residents, who organised informal sessions and matches.

In a report published by ESPNcricinfo in July 2001, journalist Ouadia Kabbaj described efforts to expand the sport beyond those communities and establish a more organised structure for Moroccan cricket.

The report highlighted the work of Abdur Rahman Bukhatir, the Emirati businessman associated with cricket's development in Sharjah. With his support, organisers were working to establish teams, develop facilities, recruit coaches and introduce the game to more Moroccan youngsters.

At that time, cricket was being played in Rabat, Tangier and Casablanca. The Moroccan Cricket Association was working to develop the sport, and plans for dedicated cricket grounds were part of the wider ambition.

The objective was bigger than staging a few international matches. It was to create the foundations of a sport that Moroccans themselves could play, follow and eventually represent at a higher level.

The Morocco Cup would become the most visible expression of that ambition.

Tangier takes centre stage

Tangier was selected to host the tournament at the National Cricket Stadium, a purpose-built venue that gave international cricket a home in Morocco.

The ground became the setting for seven one-day international matches between Pakistan, South Africa and Sri Lanka from 12 to 21 August 2002.

For a city better known internationally for its location at the meeting point of the Mediterranean Sea and the Atlantic Ocean, hosting leading cricket nations was an unusual sporting milestone.

The tournament also gave Morocco an opportunity to showcase itself to cricket audiences around the world. International matches could bring attention to the country while demonstrating that cricket did not have to remain confined to its traditional strongholds.

The stadium was more than a playing surface. It represented an attempt to turn enthusiasm for the sport into something lasting.

Three international giants arrive

The participating teams brought considerable experience and star power to Tangier.

Pakistan arrived with established names such as Waqar Younis, Wasim Akram, Saeed Anwar and Inzamam-ul-Haq. South Africa featured players including Shaun Pollock, Allan Donald, Jacques Kallis, Jonty Rhodes and Herschelle Gibbs. Sri Lanka brought Sanath Jayasuriya, Muttiah Muralitharan, Kumar Sangakkara and Mahela Jayawardene.

These were not unknown teams playing an exhibition. The tournament was contested through official 50-over international matches, with a final to decide the champion.

For spectators in Tangier, it was an opportunity to see international cricket played at a level rarely associated with North Africa at the time.

For Morocco, it was a chance to occupy a place on the international cricket calendar.

Sri Lanka emerge as champions

The Morocco Cup began on 12 August 2002, when South Africa defeated Pakistan by 54 runs. The competition continued with six league matches before the final on 21 August.

Sri Lanka proved the strongest side in the tournament. Their captain, Sanath Jayasuriya, was a major influence with the bat, while the team's bowling attack included the legendary Muttiah Muralitharan.

In the final against South Africa, Sri Lanka scored 235 for seven in their 50 overs. Jayasuriya led the way with an outstanding 71 from 71 balls, while Kumar Sangakkara contributed 40.

South Africa fought back through Mark Boucher, who scored 70, and Boeta Dippenaar, who made 53. Their partnership revived the chase after South Africa had lost six wickets for 91 runs.

However, Sri Lanka held their nerve and dismissed South Africa for 208 in 48.3 overs, winning the final by 27 runs.

Jayasuriya was named Player of the Match. The result completed a memorable tournament victory for Sri Lanka in Tangier.

The scorecard preserved the details of the final, but the wider significance extended beyond the runs and wickets. Morocco had hosted an international tournament featuring three major cricket nations and some of the era's best-known players.

More than a tournament

The Morocco Cup was an international sporting event, but the ambition surrounding Moroccan cricket reached further.

The development plans described before the tournament included organised teams, coaching, local competitions and the introduction of cricket into schools. These were important ingredients for building a sport beyond a single high-profile event.

An international tournament can attract attention, but sustainable growth depends on what follows: accessible playing facilities, regular competition, trained coaches, youth participation and opportunities for local players.

The 2001 reporting on Moroccan cricket documented the existence of organised teams and development efforts before the Morocco Cup took place. It is an important reminder that the country's cricket story was not simply about bringing overseas stars to Tangier.

There were people working to establish the game locally, too.

What happened after Tangier?

The Morocco Cup remains a striking chapter in the country's cricket history. But staging international matches and building a lasting domestic cricket structure are two different challenges.

The tournament itself does not tell us everything about what happened to the ground, the teams or the development programmes in the years that followed. Those questions deserve further investigation using reliable historical records and accounts from people connected with Moroccan cricket.

What is clear is that the tournament happened, the international scorecards survive, and the ambitions for organised cricket in Morocco were being discussed even before the event began.

Understanding the years after 2002 is just as important as celebrating the tournament. It can help explain which efforts succeeded, which challenges remained and what lessons future development initiatives might draw from the experience.

Morocco's cricket history should include both its achievements and the unanswered questions.

Why this story matters today

At Morocco Cricket, we believe the story of Tangier deserves to be rediscovered.

It challenges the assumption that cricket belongs only to countries where it has been played for generations. It shows that Morocco has already been part of an international cricket story — one involving famous players, competitive matches and an ambitious vision for the sport.

But history should be more than nostalgia.

The real opportunity is to use this story to inspire a new generation of Moroccan players, coaches, teachers, volunteers and supporters. The next chapter should not depend only on international teams visiting Morocco. It should be about creating opportunities for Moroccan youngsters to learn the game, play regularly and represent their country.

The 2002 Morocco Cup demonstrated that international cricket could be staged in Tangier. The longer-term challenge is to build the foundations that allow the game to thrive locally.

Morocco has already welcomed the world of cricket once. The question now is how the next chapter can be written by Moroccan players themselves.
